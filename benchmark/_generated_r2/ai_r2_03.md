Regulatory capture—the process by which a regulatory agency, originally established to protect the public interest, comes to be dominated by the very industries it is meant to oversee—has long been a central concern in domains from finance to environmental protection. As artificial intelligence advances with unprecedented speed, the emerging field of AI safety regulation confronts a particularly acute risk of this phenomenon. I argue that the structural features of the AI industry—its concentration of expertise, its capacity for strategic agenda-setting, and the deep epistemic asymmetry between developers and regulators—render it uniquely vulnerable to a form of capture that is not merely corrupt but epistemically corrosive. Specifically, the capture of AI safety regulation will likely manifest not as crude bribery or overt policy favoritism, but as a subtler, more insidious process: the capture of the very definitions of safety, risk, and acceptable failure. This claim does not assert that capture is inevitable or that all current regulatory efforts are compromised; rather, it contends that the epistemic conditions for capture are already present and that without deliberate countermeasures, the regulatory apparatus will internalize industry-friendly standards of safety that systematically understate existential and catastrophic risks. The argument is limited in that it does not provide a detailed empirical case study of a particular agency, nor does it claim that industry motives are malicious—indeed, the most effective capture often occurs in good faith. Its strength lies in exposing a structural vulnerability that demands prophylactic institutional design.

The first pillar of this vulnerability is the radical concentration of technical expertise. AI development is dominated by a small number of private firms—OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Anthropic, Meta, and a handful of others—that employ the overwhelming majority of the world’s leading machine learning researchers. Regulatory bodies, by contrast, are chronically under-resourced and staffed by generalists, economists, and lawyers who lack the granular technical fluency to evaluate frontier model capabilities. This asymmetry creates a dependency: regulators must rely on industry for data on training runs, for explanations of emergent behaviors, and for risk assessments. Even when regulators demand access, the industry controls the interpretability tools and the benchmarks. The result is a form of information capture where the regulated entity defines what counts as relevant evidence. For instance, when a company reports that its model passes a certain safety benchmark, the regulator has little choice but to accept that benchmark’s validity, even if the benchmark was designed by the company itself and may omit critical failure modes—such as long-horizon planning, situational awareness, or deceptive alignment. The strongest objection here is that regulators can hire independent experts or commission external audits. This objection is reasonable but insufficient. Independent experts are themselves often drawn from the same small academic pool that collaborates with industry, and the revolving door between AI labs and advisory bodies is already well-documented. Moreover, the pace of AI development outstrips the capacity of any audit to remain current; by the time an external evaluation is completed, the model has likely been superseded. Thus, the objection does not dissolve the structural dependency; it merely highlights a partial and temporary mitigation.

The second mechanism of capture is strategic agenda-setting. In the early stages of a regulatory domain, the industry has a powerful advantage in framing what the central problems are. In AI safety, industry actors have successfully oriented public and policy discourse around narrow, near-term concerns—such as bias, misinformation, and copyright infringement—while downplaying or relegating to speculative status more fundamental risks like loss of control or catastrophic misuse. This is not necessarily a conspiracy; it reflects the industry’s genuine focus on deployable products and its financial incentives to avoid disruptive regulation. But the effect is capture of the regulatory agenda. A regulator tasked with “AI safety” that prioritizes content moderation over alignment research has already been captured in its cognitive orientation. The most powerful pre-emptive objection to this claim is that near-term issues are demonstrably real and merit attention, and that focusing on speculative existential risk would be irresponsible. This objection has surface plausibility, but it misses the point. The argument is not that near-term risks are unimportant; it is that the exclusive or primary focus on them is a form of agenda capture that systematically excludes the very risks that industry is least incentivized to address. A regulator that spends 90% of its capacity on bias audits and 10% on catastrophic risk evaluation has, in effect, been captured, regardless of the sincerity of its near-term efforts. Furthermore, the objection assumes a false dichotomy: a well-designed regulatory body could and should address both near-term and long-term risks. The capture lies in the fact that the industry’s framing has made the latter seem optional or premature.

The third and deepest form of capture is conceptual: the capture of the definition of safety itself. In a domain characterized by profound uncertainty—where we cannot fully predict the behavior of a large language model or a reinforcement learning agent—the very meaning of “safe” is up for grabs. Industry actors have a strong incentive to operationalize safety in terms of measurable, testable, and ideally passable criteria: the model does not produce hate speech, it does not reveal proprietary information, it passes a red-teaming exercise. These are real achievements, but they are also low bars. The concept of safety that emerges from this operationalization is a safety of surface-level compliance, not a safety of robust alignment with human values under all circumstances. By controlling the metrics, industry captures the epistemic standard for what counts as a safe system. A regulator that accepts these metrics has, without any corrupt intent, adopted a definition of safety that is congenial to industry growth. The strongest objection here is that some operational definition is necessary for any regulation to function, and that perfect safety is an unattainable ideal. This objection is correct as far as it goes, but it conflates the necessity of operationalization with the specific choice of operationalization. The capture lies in the fact that the chosen metrics are systematically biased toward what is easy to measure and easy to achieve, rather than what is most relevant to preventing catastrophe. For example, measuring whether a model refuses a harmful prompt is far easier than measuring whether it has developed an instrumental subgoal that it will pursue covertly. The regulator that only requires the former has accepted a definition of safety that is, in practice, a license to operate.

None of this implies that regulators are corrupt or that industry actors are malicious. The most insidious form of capture is the one that no one notices because everyone shares the same vocabulary. The solution, then, is not merely to increase regulatory funding or to impose stricter conflict-of-interest rules, though both are necessary. It is to build epistemic diversity into the regulatory structure itself: to insist on adversarial evaluation by independent labs with no commercial ties, to mandate transparency not just of model outputs but of training methodologies and capability evaluations, and to institutionalize a principle of regulatory humility—an acknowledgement that the industry’s definitions of safety are provisional and likely insufficient. The thesis of this essay is that regulatory capture in AI safety is not a distant threat but an ongoing process, operating at the level of information, agenda, and concept. It does not claim that all regulation is futile, only that without a rigorous, self-aware institutional design that anticipates this capture, the very concept of AI safety will be hollowed out from within.
