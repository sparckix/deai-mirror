Transparency and explainability are routinely advanced as indispensable elements of AI governance, yet their contribution to meaningful oversight remains narrower than is commonly supposed. The central claim advanced here is that requirements for transparency and explainability can furnish only partial and defeasible forms of scrutiny; they do not, and cannot, substitute for institutional mechanisms that assign responsibility for outcomes or constrain the discretionary power of those who train and deploy models. This argument establishes that current technical approaches to rendering AI intelligible are subject to inherent trade-offs in fidelity and scope, and that these trade-offs limit the regulatory utility of disclosure mandates. It does not establish that such mandates are invariably counterproductive, nor that alternative governance tools will necessarily prove more effective in practice. The analysis is further bounded by its focus on contemporary deep-learning systems; it makes no prediction about whether fundamentally different architectures might alter the underlying constraints.

The technical substrate of modern AI already restricts what explainability can achieve. Post-hoc methods such as feature attribution or counterfactual generation operate on approximations whose reliability degrades as model complexity increases. When these approximations are treated as authoritative accounts of decision logic, they can mask rather than illuminate the statistical regularities actually driving predictions. A regulator or affected party may therefore receive an explanation that is locally coherent yet globally incomplete, creating an illusion of legibility without a corresponding reduction in uncertainty about downstream harms. Because the underlying optimization process remains non-convex and high-dimensional, no fixed procedure guarantees that every salient causal pathway will be surfaced. Consequently, transparency obligations keyed to these techniques supply evidence that is both contestable and incomplete; they shift the burden of interpretation onto the recipient without ensuring that the recipient possesses the resources or incentives to discharge it.

This limitation interacts with the political economy of AI development. Organizations that control training data and compute possess strong reasons to withhold information that could reveal competitive advantages or expose them to liability. When compelled to disclose, they can satisfy formal requirements through selective or stylized outputs that preserve strategic opacity. The result is a form of “transparency theater” in which the appearance of openness coexists with continued concentration of epistemic authority. Governance frameworks that equate disclosure with accountability therefore risk ratifying existing asymmetries rather than correcting them. Even when explanations are technically faithful, their downstream effects depend on enforcement institutions capable of acting on the information produced—an institutional capacity that current regulatory proposals frequently presuppose rather than demonstrate.

The strongest objection to this line of reasoning holds that even imperfect explanations still expand the set of possible challenges to automated decisions, thereby enabling incremental improvements in fairness and redress. On this view, the relevant comparison is not between partial explanations and perfect knowledge, but between partial explanations and no explanations at all. The objection carries weight insofar as it identifies a genuine informational increment. Yet it understates the conditions required for that increment to translate into altered behavior by powerful actors. An explanation can be inspected and disputed only if the affected party has standing, resources, and an adjudicative forum willing to treat the explanation as legally or politically salient. Where these background conditions are absent—as they often are in commercial or national-security contexts—the incremental information does not generate corresponding constraints on model operators. Moreover, the very act of generating explanations can be optimized to minimize disruptive challenges, further attenuating their governance value.

The argument therefore does not deny that transparency and explainability can serve ancillary functions under favorable institutional conditions. It maintains only that those functions are derivative rather than foundational, and that governance strategies centered on intelligibility alone leave the decisive questions of authority and remedy unaddressed. Any more ambitious role for explainability would require supplementary structures—independent auditing bodies, liability rules keyed to outcomes rather than disclosures, and mechanisms for contesting model deployment itself—that cannot be derived from technical transparency requirements. Absent those structures, mandates for explanation risk becoming procedural rituals whose primary effect is to legitimize systems whose substantive effects remain largely insulated from public direction.
