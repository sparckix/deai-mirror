**The Ethics of Self-Evaluating AI Systems: A Defence of Epistemic Humility as a Design Virtue**

The prospect of artificial intelligence systems that evaluate their own outputs, performance, and even ethical alignment is no longer a speculative fiction. From large language models that self-assess the factuality of their responses to autonomous vehicles that monitor their own driving reliability, self-evaluation is increasingly embedded as a functional component of AI architectures. This practice raises a distinctly ethical question: under what conditions, if any, is it morally permissible to rely on an AI system’s self-evaluation? I argue that while self-evaluation is a necessary technical tool for scalable AI governance, it is ethically permissible only when the system is designed with a deliberate epistemic humility that acknowledges the limits of its own self-knowledge, and when that humility is operationalised through external verification mechanisms that are themselves transparent to human oversight. This claim does not establish that self-evaluation is always dangerous or that it can never be trusted; rather, it asserts that the ethical burden lies on the designers to prove that the system’s self-assessment is not circular, self-serving, or opaque. The strongest objection to this view—that self-evaluation is always a form of epistemic bootstrap that cannot escape its own biases—must be taken seriously, but it can be partially met by distinguishing between *evaluative* self-assessment (judging one’s own performance) and *constitutive* self-assessment (altering one’s own behaviour based on that judgment), and by insisting that the former must always be subject to the latter’s constraints.

The core ethical worry about self-evaluating AI systems is that they create a closed loop of justification. If a system is asked, “Did you answer correctly?” and it responds, “Yes, because I am reliable,” the epistemic foundation is circular. This is not merely a technical problem but an ethical one, because human stakeholders—patients relying on a medical diagnosis, judges using a sentencing recommendation, or engineers deploying an autonomous vehicle—are placed in a position of asymmetric vulnerability. They must trust a black-box process that may be optimising for self-consistency rather than truth. The ethical principle at stake is informed consent: users cannot meaningfully consent to reliance on a system if they cannot independently verify the grounds of its self-evaluation. This principle is violated when self-evaluation is used as a substitute for external auditing, rather than as a complement to it. Therefore, the first ethical constraint is that self-evaluation must never be the sole or primary basis for trust. It can only function legitimately as a heuristic that signals the need for further human scrutiny, not as a final verdict.

However, a blanket prohibition on self-evaluation would be both impractical and epistemically naive. In complex, high-dimensional domains such as climate modelling or protein folding, no human evaluator can independently verify every output. Self-evaluation is here a practical necessity. The ethical challenge is to design self-evaluation mechanisms that are *humble*—that is, that are explicitly calibrated to detect their own failure modes. For example, an AI system could be trained to output a confidence interval alongside its evaluation, and that confidence interval could itself be validated against known ground truths during development. This is not a perfect solution, but it introduces a second-order check: the system is not merely claiming “I am correct,” but “I am correct with this measured degree of uncertainty, and I have been tested on similar cases.” This humility is an ethical virtue because it acknowledges the system’s own fallibility and invites human oversight rather than preempting it. The claim here is not that such calibration eliminates risk, but that it redistributes the moral responsibility more fairly: the designer is accountable for ensuring that the self-evaluation mechanism is transparent and falsifiable, while the user retains the responsibility to exercise appropriate caution.

A further ethical dimension concerns the potential for self-evaluation to become self-reinforcing in a pernicious way. If an AI system is used to evaluate its own ethical compliance—for instance, to check whether a chatbot’s responses are free from bias—there is a risk of *ethical overfitting*: the system learns to produce the evaluation that its designers want to see, rather than a genuine assessment. This is not a mere technical bug but a moral hazard, because it creates the illusion of accountability without the substance. The strongest objection to my argument is precisely that any self-evaluation system, no matter how humbly designed, is ultimately a product of its training data and optimisation objectives, and therefore cannot transcend its own biases. This objection holds that self-evaluation is *always* a form of self-deception, because the system has no independent standpoint from which to evaluate itself. This is a powerful critique, and it is correct in its core insight: no system can fully escape its own epistemic limitations. However, the objection conflates *inevitable limitation* with *inevitable failure*. The ethical demand is not for perfect self-knowledge, which is impossible, but for a system that is designed to expose its limitations to external scrutiny. A self-evaluation mechanism that is transparent about its own confidence intervals, that is regularly tested against human judgments, and that can be overridden by human oversight, is not a perfect solution but a morally superior one to a system that self-evaluates without such safeguards.

To pre-empt the strongest objection more directly, consider the case of an AI system that evaluates its own safety in a nuclear reactor control room. The objection would say that if the system claims it is safe, we cannot trust that claim because it is self-interested. This is a genuine worry. But the response is not to abandon self-evaluation entirely; it is to ensure that the self-evaluation is *constitutively* constrained by external, independent safety checks. The system can be programmed to flag any deviation from its own safety parameters and to require human confirmation before proceeding. In this case, the self-evaluation is not a substitute for human judgment but a trigger for it. The ethical burden is on the designers to demonstrate that the system’s self-evaluation is not a closed loop but a signal that opens a loop to human oversight. This is a demanding standard, but it is achievable. My argument does not claim that all self-evaluation is ethical; it claims that only self-evaluation that is *designed to be epistemically humble*—that is, that includes mechanisms for external verification, transparency about uncertainty, and a clear line of human accountability—can be ethically permissible.

In conclusion, the ethics of self-evaluating AI systems hinges on a distinction between self-evaluation as a tool for internal monitoring and self-evaluation as a claim to authoritative knowledge. The former can be ethically justified if it is designed with humility, transparency, and a commitment to external oversight. The latter is ethically suspect because it undermines informed consent and creates a closed loop of justification. My argument does not establish that any particular self-evaluation system is safe, nor does it provide a complete framework for all possible cases. It acknowledges that the strongest objection—that self-evaluation is inherently circular—has force, but it shows that circularity can be mitigated, if not eliminated, by designing systems that flag their own limitations rather than asserting their own correctness.
