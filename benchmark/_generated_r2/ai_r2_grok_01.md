The enterprise of auditing artificial intelligence systems is frequently presented as a mechanism for rendering opaque technical artifacts amenable to external scrutiny. Yet this framing overstates what auditing can achieve. AI auditing, understood as the systematic evaluation of a model’s properties against predefined criteria through testing, documentation review, or behavioral observation, is constitutively unable to deliver comprehensive assurances of safety, fairness, or legal compliance. It can generate evidence about performance on selected metrics within bounded test distributions, but it cannot establish that a system will behave acceptably across the open-ended contexts in which it will actually operate. This limitation follows from the interaction of three structural features: the non-exhaustiveness of any finite test regime, the underspecification of the normative criteria being audited, and the capacity of deployed models to exhibit emergent behaviors that escape prior evaluation regimes.

Any audit necessarily samples from a vastly larger space of possible inputs and interactions. Even when auditors employ adversarial or stress-testing methods, they confront a combinatorial explosion that no practical procedure can traverse. A model may satisfy fairness constraints on a curated dataset while producing systematically different outputs once covariate shifts or strategic responses from users alter the input distribution. Because the audit must terminate, the absence of detected failures supplies only an absence of evidence within the sampled slice, not evidence of absence across the unsampled remainder. This epistemic gap is not merely practical; it is produced by the fact that modern machine-learning systems are optimized for statistical generalization rather than for exhaustive specification of edge cases. Consequently, an audit can falsify certain narrow claims of reliability but cannot verify the broader claim that unacceptable behavior will not appear later.

The difficulty is compounded by the normative criteria themselves. Notions such as “fairness,” “transparency,” or “safety” resist reduction to stable, machine-checkable properties. Different stakeholders reasonably disagree about which operationalization should govern an audit, and any single choice excludes alternative conceptions that might have flagged different risks. An auditor who certifies compliance with one definition of demographic parity thereby remains silent on whether that definition adequately captures the relevant moral or legal concern. The resulting certificate therefore attests only to conformity with an operational proxy, not to the satisfaction of the underlying normative demand. Because the proxy is chosen rather than discovered, the audit cannot close the gap between measurable performance and contested value.

These constraints do not imply that auditing is pointless. Targeted audits can still surface specific, actionable defects and can contribute to iterative improvement when embedded within broader governance arrangements that include ongoing monitoring and liability rules. The argument advanced here establishes only that auditing cannot function as a standalone guarantor of acceptable system behavior; it does not establish that auditing lacks instrumental value within a more pluralistic accountability regime.

A natural objection holds that the limits described are contingent on present techniques and will recede as auditing methods mature. Continuous or “living” audits, red-teaming at larger scales, and formal verification of restricted subsystems might, on this view, progressively shrink the space of unexamined behaviors until the residual uncertainty becomes negligible. The objection is serious because it correctly identifies that auditing practices can improve. Yet it underestimates the persistence of underspecification. Even an indefinitely extended audit must still select which behaviors to monitor and which normative translations to adopt; those selections remain contestable and incomplete relative to the full range of future deployments. Moreover, once a model is placed in an environment containing other adaptive agents, new interaction effects can arise that were not present during any pre-deployment audit, however prolonged. Formal methods, for their part, apply only to properties that can be stated in advance with mathematical precision, leaving aside the very normative and contextual judgments that generate the hardest auditing problems. Thus, while auditing techniques may become more informative, they do not escape the fundamental sampling and specification constraints that prevent them from furnishing exhaustive verification.

The implication is that reliance on audit outcomes for high-stakes decisions requires supplementary mechanisms—such as usage restrictions, post-deployment redress procedures, and explicit allocations of residual risk—that do not presuppose the possibility of complete prior certification.
