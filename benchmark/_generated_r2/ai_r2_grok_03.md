Regulatory capture describes the process by which regulated entities shape the rules intended to constrain them, often converting public-interest mandates into barriers that entrench incumbents. In the domain of artificial intelligence safety, this dynamic is frequently invoked to explain why proposed oversight mechanisms might ultimately serve the commercial interests of leading laboratories rather than reduce existential or societal-scale risks. The present argument holds that capture is indeed a structural risk in AI governance, yet the technical opacity and rapid iteration characteristic of frontier model development impose epistemic constraints that prevent capture from reproducing the stable, low-ambition equilibria observed in more mature industries. This claim establishes only that certain features of the technology make thoroughgoing capture more difficult to sustain than standard public-choice models predict; it does not demonstrate that meaningful safety regulation will emerge, nor that any regulation adopted will prove effective against the most severe hazards. The analysis remains theoretical and analogical, given the absence of settled regulatory regimes for general-purpose AI systems.

The standard mechanism of capture relies on information asymmetries that allow industry to supply both the data and the interpretive frameworks regulators require. In pharmaceuticals or finance, firms possess proprietary knowledge of production processes and risk models that agencies cannot replicate at comparable speed or granularity. AI laboratories enjoy similar asymmetries, yet the underlying uncertainty is deeper: no laboratory currently possesses reliable methods for measuring or forecasting the alignment properties of systems that do not yet exist. When regulators seek metrics for “dangerous capability” or “deceptive alignment,” laboratories can supply benchmarks, but those benchmarks rest on assumptions whose validity is contested even within the technical community. This shared epistemic fragility limits the capacity of any single actor to lock in a permanently favorable interpretation of compliance. A regulator captured by today’s leading firms would still confront the possibility that tomorrow’s architectural shift renders its own standards obsolete or counterproductive, thereby exposing the agency to subsequent political sanction.

A further constraint arises from the distribution of relevant expertise. Unlike nuclear engineering or aviation, where domain knowledge concentrates inside a small number of organizations with long institutional histories, machine-learning research remains dispersed across universities, smaller startups, and independent laboratories. Although a handful of firms currently command the largest training runs, the conceptual tools used to evaluate safety properties circulate more widely. Any attempt to embed lax standards into statute or agency guidance must therefore survive scrutiny from researchers whose career incentives are not aligned with the largest commercial players. This dispersion does not eliminate lobbying advantages, but it raises the transaction costs of achieving durable consensus around weak rules.

These observations do not imply that capture is impossible or that laboratories lack incentive to pursue it. They indicate only that the usual feedback loop—industry supplies the expertise, regulators adopt industry-framed standards, standards raise entry costs—is interrupted by persistent technical disagreement over what constitutes evidence of safety. Historical cases of capture presuppose domains in which the central hazards can be rendered legible to bureaucratic routines; AI safety has not yet reached that stage.

The strongest objection holds that these epistemic features are transient. Once a dominant paradigm stabilizes and leading laboratories accumulate proprietary evaluation methods, the familiar pattern of informational dependence will reassert itself, allowing capture to proceed along conventional lines. This objection correctly identifies that technological maturity tends to reduce uncertainty and thereby facilitate regulatory entrenchment. Yet it understates the speed at which new architectures and scaling regimes continue to appear. Even if one laboratory achieves temporary dominance in evaluation techniques, the next generation of models is likely to exhibit capabilities that render prior metrics incomplete. Under such conditions, regulators face repeated demands to revise standards before any single set of rules can harden into a durable barrier protecting incumbents. The objection therefore describes a possible future equilibrium rather than an assured trajectory; reaching that equilibrium would require a slowdown in fundamental progress that has not been observed to date.

The argument acknowledges its own limits. It offers no quantitative estimate of lobbying expenditures, no model of agency budget constraints, and no prediction about which jurisdictions will adopt rules first. It relies on the premise that technical disagreement will persist at a level sufficient to frustrate consensus, a premise that could be falsified by unexpected theoretical convergence. Finally, the analysis brackets the possibility that governments might deliberately delegate standard-setting authority to the very entities whose behavior is at issue, a move that would bypass epistemic constraints altogether. Within these boundaries, the claim is simply that the distinctive character of AI development supplies a partial, time-limited obstacle to the most complete forms of regulatory capture.
