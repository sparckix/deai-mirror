Accountability in AI decision-making has become a central concern as algorithmic systems increasingly govern outcomes in domains ranging from criminal justice and credit scoring to medical diagnosis and hiring. The dominant response to this challenge is a demand for transparency: the idea that if we can see inside the “black box” of a model, we can assign responsibility for its decisions. While intuitively appealing, this approach conflates two distinct concepts—explainability and accountability—and, in doing so, risks creating a false sense of ethical closure. I argue that meaningful accountability in AI cannot be achieved through transparency alone, but requires a shift from explainability to answerability: a relational and institutional framework in which designated human actors bear clear, enforceable obligations for the consequences of algorithmic outputs, irrespective of whether those outputs are fully interpretable. This claim does not deny the value of transparency; it merely insists that transparency is a necessary but insufficient condition for accountability. It does not propose a single institutional model, nor does it claim that answerability resolves all ethical tensions inherent in automated decision-making. What it does establish is that the current fixation on opening the black box distracts from the more fundamental task of designing governance structures that embed responsibility in human agents who can be called to account.

The thesis that transparency is insufficient rests on a precise distinction between explaining a decision and being answerable for it. An explainable AI system might reveal that a loan application was denied because the applicant’s zip code correlates with a high default rate. This explanation may satisfy a technical requirement for interpretability, but it does not, by itself, tell us who is responsible for using that feature, who should be blamed for its discriminatory effects, or who must remedy the harm. The explanation merely describes the causal chain within the model; it does not assign moral or legal liability. Accountability, in contrast, implies a relationship in which an actor has a duty to justify their actions to a specific forum and faces consequences—whether reputational, financial, or legal—if that justification fails. Transparency, even if perfect, cannot generate this relationship. It can only provide the raw material for accountability. Without a designated human agent who has the authority to override the model, the obligation to monitor its outcomes, and the exposure to sanctions for failures, the most transparent AI system remains an unaccountable one. The current push for algorithmic audits and model cards, while valuable, often assumes that once a system is legible, accountability will naturally follow. This assumption is mistaken: legibility creates the possibility of scrutiny, but scrutiny without a mechanism for redress is a spectator sport.

Acknowledging the limits of this argument is essential. The claim that answerability is the core of accountability does not specify what form that answerability should take in practice. It might involve a human-in-the-loop who reviews high-stakes decisions, a regulatory body that certifies algorithms before deployment, or a liability regime that holds deploying organizations strictly responsible for harms. The argument is agnostic among these mechanisms, and it does not claim that any single design is universally optimal. Furthermore, the argument does not resolve the problem of distributed responsibility. In complex AI supply chains—where data is collected by one firm, models trained by another, and systems deployed by a third—it may be genuinely difficult to identify which human agent is answerable for a given failure. The argument acknowledges that answerability requires a clear chain of command, and that such chains are often absent in contemporary AI development. This is not a refutation of the thesis, but rather a practical obstacle that underscores the need for legal and organizational reform. Finally, the argument does not claim that explainability is irrelevant. On the contrary, effective answerability often depends on the ability of the accountable party to understand why a decision was made, so that they can defend it or adjust it. The point is simply that explainability is a tool for accountability, not a substitute for it.

The strongest objection to this thesis is that answerability without transparency is empty, and that my insistence on separating the two underestimates the extent to which opacity itself undermines accountability. The critic might argue that if a decision-maker cannot explain why a model reached a particular conclusion—for example, in the case of deep neural networks whose internal representations are inscrutable—then it is impossible for that decision-maker to justify the decision to an affected party. How can a doctor be answerable for a diagnosis made by a black-box model if the doctor cannot articulate the reasoning behind it? How can a judge be held accountable for a sentencing recommendation if the algorithm’s logic is hidden? In this view, transparency is not merely a precondition for accountability; it is constitutive of it. Without explanation, answerability collapses into a mere ritual of blame, where a human is punished for outcomes they could not reasonably have prevented or understood.

This objection is powerful, and it demands a careful response. First, I concede that in some contexts—particularly those involving individual rights and high-stakes decisions—a minimum level of transparency is necessary for answerability to be meaningful. A judge cannot be answerable for a sentence if they cannot explain the factors that influenced it, because the affected party has a right to know the basis of the decision. However, this does not require that the model itself be fully interpretable. It requires that the human decision-maker have access to a sufficiently informative proxy explanation—such as feature attribution scores or counterfactual examples—that allows them to give a coherent account of their decision to the affected party. The model’s internal opacity can be managed through external validation: the doctor does not need to understand every weight in the neural network; they need to know that the model’s recommendation aligns with clinical evidence, that it has been tested on relevant populations, and that they can override it when it conflicts with their professional judgment. Answerability, in this view, is not about reproducing the model’s internal logic, but about providing a justification that is socially and legally acceptable. Second, the objection assumes that transparency automatically produces answerability, when in fact the two are often decoupled. A fully transparent decision—such as a rule-based system that clearly states its criteria—can still be unaccountable if no one is empowered to challenge or change it. The history of bureaucratic decision-making shows that transparency without answerability can lead to a “technocratic alibi,” where officials hide behind rules they claim they cannot alter. Answerability requires not just visibility, but a structure of oversight, appeal, and remedy.

Ultimately, the pursuit of accountability in AI must resist the seduction of the black box as a singular problem to be solved. The real challenge is not that algorithms are opaque, but that they are often orphaned: designed and deployed without clear lines of human responsibility. By reframing accountability as answerability, we foreground the need for institutional design—for creating roles, procedures, and sanctions that ensure someone is always on the hook for the machine’s outputs. This does not diminish the importance of explainability, but it subordinates it to a more fundamental ethical and political task: ensuring that power, even when exercised through code, remains answerable to those it affects.
