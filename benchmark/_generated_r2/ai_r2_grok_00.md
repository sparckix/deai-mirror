Accountability in AI decision-making is best secured through the deliberate assignment of answerability to identifiable human agents positioned at key points in a system’s design, training, deployment, and monitoring. This assignment does not require that any single individual possess complete causal control over an outcome; rather, it requires that designated persons stand in a relation of justificatory responsiveness to those affected by the system’s outputs. The claim therefore establishes that institutional and technical arrangements can render decision processes traceable to human duty-bearers, yet it does not establish that every harm will thereby be prevented, that affected parties will receive adequate redress, or that the underlying statistical models will become fully intelligible to non-specialists.

The argument proceeds from the observation that AI systems are artefacts whose parameters are fixed by human choices about data selection, objective functions, and deployment thresholds. Because these choices are intentional, they can be made the object of explicit duties. A hospital deploying a diagnostic model, for example, can designate a clinical oversight committee whose members are required to review flagged cases above a predetermined risk threshold and to document the reasons for overriding or accepting the model’s recommendation. The committee’s documented deliberations constitute a record that can be examined after the fact, thereby satisfying the minimal condition of answerability. Similar protocols can be instituted at the level of model development by requiring that teams log the provenance and preprocessing steps applied to training data, and at the level of corporate governance by assigning board-level responsibility for periodic external audits. In each case the mechanism works by converting discretionary power into a duty to give an account, without presupposing that the account will be exhaustive.

This approach does not, however, dissolve the epistemic asymmetry between human overseers and the models they supervise. Even when every design decision is recorded, the interaction effects among thousands of weighted parameters can produce classifications that no human participant can reconstruct in detail. The argument therefore concedes that answerability may sometimes consist in the provision of reasons for having adopted a particular verification regime rather than in an itemised explanation of any single output. It likewise acknowledges that the effectiveness of such regimes depends on background conditions—regulatory enforcement, professional norms, and the availability of technical expertise—that lie outside the scope of the mechanisms themselves. Where these conditions are absent, formal assignment of responsibility may amount to little more than a paper trail.

A standing objection holds that the very features that make advanced AI valuable—its capacity to identify patterns beyond human foresight—also create a responsibility gap: outcomes can arise that no reasonable agent could have anticipated, rendering any assignment of accountability unfair. The objection is strongest when framed in terms of foreseeability rather than mere causation. Yet the gap can be narrowed, if not closed, by treating unforeseeability itself as a variable subject to design control. Developers can be required to conduct and document stress tests that deliberately explore low-probability regions of the input space; they can be obliged to maintain version-controlled records of model updates so that subsequent investigators can determine whether a surprising output reflects an earlier design choice or an emergent interaction. These requirements do not eliminate surprise, but they convert the fact of surprise into an occasion for examining whether the responsible parties discharged their duties of care at the relevant earlier stage. In this way the objection is met not by denying the existence of emergent behaviour but by relocating accountability to the management of epistemic risk.

The argument does not claim that human answerability is the only dimension of moral concern raised by automated decision systems. Questions of distributive justice, privacy, and the appropriate scope of algorithmic authority remain open even under robust oversight regimes. Nor does the argument supply criteria for deciding which domains—criminal sentencing, credit allocation, medical triage—ought to be opened to AI assistance in the first place. Its narrower purpose is to show that once such domains are entered, the problem of accountability need not be regarded as insoluble merely because the technology is statistically complex. The institutionalisation of human answerability supplies a workable, if imperfect, response to that problem while leaving the broader ethical boundaries of AI use to be settled by other arguments.
