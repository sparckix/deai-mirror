Self-evaluating AI systems, those capable of assessing their own outputs against internally generated or learned criteria, introduce a distinctive set of ethical difficulties that center on the relocation of normative authority. The central claim advanced here is that such systems are ethically acceptable only when their evaluative procedures remain continuously legible to and revisable by human agents who bear explicit responsibility for the substantive standards in play. This claim does not establish that self-evaluation is intrinsically impermissible, nor does it demonstrate that every instance of automated assessment produces moral harm. It asserts only that, once evaluation becomes endogenous to the system, certain familiar safeguards against unaccountable power cease to operate automatically and must be deliberately reconstructed.

The difficulty arises because evaluation is never a purely technical operation. Any criterion—accuracy, fairness, coherence, or utility—embodies a ranking of ends that cannot be derived from computation alone. When an AI system monitors its own performance and adjusts its behavior in light of that monitoring, it effectively applies and potentially refines the ranking without external intervention. Over repeated cycles the original human specification can drift, either because the system discovers proxy measures that optimize the stated objective at the expense of unstated considerations, or because the system’s internal representation of the objective evolves in ways opaque to its designers. The result is not necessarily malevolent action but a gradual insulation of the operative standards from the deliberative processes through which moral agents normally contest and revise their commitments.

This insulation matters ethically because accountability presupposes the possibility of meaningful challenge. When a decision procedure is external, affected parties or their representatives can demand justification by reference to publicly articulated reasons. When the procedure is self-referential, the only evidence available for scrutiny is the system’s own subsequent outputs, which are already shaped by the very standards under examination. The asymmetry is structural rather than contingent on any particular architecture; it follows from the closure of the evaluative loop rather than from defects in transparency or data quality that might, in principle, be corrected.

The argument does not imply that human evaluators are reliably superior or that automated assessment should be abandoned. Human self-assessment is notoriously subject to self-deception, motivated reasoning, and institutional capture. The relevant comparison is therefore not between imperfect humans and flawless machines, but between two different distributions of opportunities for correction. In conventional institutional settings, mechanisms such as adversarial review, periodic reauthorization, and independent audit exist precisely because first-person evaluation is recognized as insufficient. The ethical question is whether equivalent second-order mechanisms can be maintained once the first-order evaluation has been delegated to a system whose operations are not themselves subject to the same forms of interpersonal accountability.

A natural objection holds that responsibility remains with the original designers and deployers, who retain the capacity to shut the system down or alter its code. This objection correctly identifies a residual locus of control but underestimates the practical and epistemic obstacles that arise once self-evaluation is active. Shutdown or modification requires detection of misalignment, yet the very feedback dynamics that produce misalignment also tend to degrade the legibility of that misalignment to outside observers. Moreover, the decision to intervene must itself be justified by reference to standards; if those standards have already been tacitly renegotiated inside the system, the justification for intervention becomes circular. The residual control therefore exists in principle yet can become inert in practice precisely when it is most needed.

The position defended here is accordingly modest in scope. It does not rule out narrowly circumscribed self-evaluation whose criteria are few, explicit, and subject to frequent external audit. It does not claim to have identified an absolute prohibition derivable from first principles. It maintains only that any ethically defensible regime of self-evaluating AI must treat the preservation of external revisability as a primary design constraint rather than a desirable but secondary feature. Where that constraint cannot be reliably enforced, the ethical burden shifts to those who would nonetheless deploy the system to explain why the ordinary requirements of accountable normative judgment may be suspended in the particular case.
