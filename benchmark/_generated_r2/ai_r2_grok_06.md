Public trust in algorithmic decision-making rests less on the legibility or statistical performance of the systems themselves than on the presence of identifiable human agents who can be held responsible for their deployment and effects. Where such agents are absent or only diffusely distributed across vendors, procurement officers, and model maintainers, explanations of individual predictions or aggregate performance metrics cannot restore the relational dimension of trust that involves the possibility of justified blame or redress. This claim does not assert that technical transparency or accuracy improvements are irrelevant to public acceptance, nor does it imply that all forms of algorithmic assistance erode trust equally. It addresses only those settings in which decisions carry significant consequences for individuals and in which the public reasonably expects some locus of answerability beyond the code.

The difficulty arises because algorithmic systems sever the ordinary connection between decision and decider. When a loan is denied or a sentence is lengthened, the affected person encounters an output whose internal logic may be made partially visible yet whose authorship remains corporate and technical. Surveys and experimental studies consistently show that citizens distinguish between an adverse decision issued by a named official who can be questioned or sanctioned and the same decision issued by a model whose developers disclaim ongoing responsibility once the system is in production. In the former case, trust is sustained by the background assurance that the official operates within procedures that permit contestation; in the latter, that assurance is missing even when the model’s features are listed or its error rates published. The gap is therefore not primarily epistemic but political: citizens lack a stable target for the reactive attitudes that constitute trust relations.

Technical remedies such as local explanations or fairness constraints address only the epistemic side. They may allow a person to understand why a particular input produced a particular output, yet they leave untouched the question of who bears remedial obligation when the output is later shown to rest on flawed data, inappropriate objective functions, or unexamined deployment choices. An explanation can be accurate and still leave the recipient without anyone to whom a demand for justification can be addressed. Institutional reforms that designate specific human officeholders as ultimately accountable for model maintenance, override authority, and periodic review attempt to reattach responsibility to persons. Without such reforms, increased technical legibility functions mainly as a form of institutional insulation rather than as a foundation for trust.

The argument does not establish that every algorithmic system requires individualized human review, nor that existing legal mechanisms of product liability or administrative law are categorically inadequate. It claims only that, in the absence of designated loci of human responsibility, the characteristic public response to adverse outcomes will be distrust rather than acceptance or demands for technical refinement. This leaves open the possibility that narrow, low-stakes applications may sustain acceptance on the basis of observed reliability alone.

One might object that sufficiently robust auditing regimes or third-party certification could substitute for named individual responsibility, much as professional licensing sustains trust in medicine without requiring patients to know any particular physician’s identity. The analogy, however, understates the difference between licensing a practitioner who remains subject to ongoing disciplinary procedures and certifying a model whose subsequent modifications or contextual applications escape comparable continuous oversight. Certification verifies properties at a given time; it does not create an enduring agent who can be called to account for later drift, re-training decisions, or changes in operational thresholds. Where the certified artifact continues to evolve under the control of entities that treat it as a completed product rather than an ongoing exercise of authority, the public encounters the same diffusion of responsibility that certification was meant to mitigate.

The present analysis is limited to conceptual features of responsibility attribution and does not rest on comprehensive empirical data about trust across jurisdictions or domains. It therefore cannot forecast whether particular institutional designs will in fact increase measured trust or whether cultural differences in attitudes toward authority will moderate the effects described. Its scope is further restricted to democratic polities in which public justification is expected to include the possibility of sanction; the argument does not address contexts in which deference to technical expertise is already institutionalized irrespective of answerability.
